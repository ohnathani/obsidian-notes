## 1. Self, Person, and Being

- **Persons are organisms**
    - A person is not merely a biological organism.
    - A person also has a **notion of self**.
    - It is important for a person to have a **sense of self**.
    - The self exists within the **realm of the symbolic**.
        - We understand ourselves through language, concepts, culture, names, roles, etc.

### Body, Self, and Person

These are **three different dimensions**:

1. **Body**
    - The physical/biological organism.
2. **Self**
    - One's sense or conception of "I."
    - How one understands oneself.
3. **Person**
    - The socially recognized individual.
    - Exists within relationships, culture, institutions, and society.

- Therefore, **body ≠ self ≠ person**.
- Personhood develops within a social world.

---

## 2. The Self Is Not Always Individualized

- Different cultures have different understandings of the self.
- Some cultures have an **intense and expansive sense of self**.
- In modern/Western contexts, our notion of self tends to be **individualized**.
    - The individual is treated as a distinct, independent self.
- But there are cultures where the self can be understood more **collectively**.
    - For example, a person may understand their **family as part of their self**.
    - The boundary between "me" and "my family" becomes less rigid.

### The Self Is Elastic

- The **self is elastic**.
    - The boundaries of the self can expand or contract.
- The self can include:
    - one's body
    - family
    - friends
    - community
    - culture
    - society

**Key idea:**

> The self is not necessarily an isolated individual. What counts as "self" depends partly on one's relationships and culture.

# 3. Personhood and Lipunan

- **Personhood is relational.**
- A person does not become a person completely independently.
- Our notion of who we are develops through the **lipunan** (society).

### How did you become a self?

- You became a self **because of the lipunan**.
- Other people help mediate your understanding of yourself.
- Relationships such as:
    
    - family
    - friends
    - community
    - institutions
    - state
    
    help constitute and recognize the person.
    

### Why are friends, family, and the state mediators?

Because our relationships with them help establish:

- who we are
- what we are allowed to do
- what is expected of us
- what responsibilities we have
- how other people recognize us

---

# 4. Lipunan and the State

- The **state is an organized society**.
- You cannot really imagine a state without **institutions**.
- Society requires networks and mechanisms that allow people to interact.

### Institutions

Institutions organize relationships between people.

For example:

- government
- schools
- banks
- courts
- markets
- families

**Possible lecture question:**

> Why is there a network if there is no means of exchanging goods?

- A society requires mechanisms for **exchange and coordination**.
- People cannot simply rely on direct personal relationships for everything.
- Institutions mediate relationships between strangers.

# 5. Money, Debt, and Trust

### Money did not simply come from barter

- The idea that money developed simply because people needed a better form of barter is incomplete.
- Money is connected to **debt and obligation**.

### Debt

- There needs to be a **mechanism of debt**.
- A debt is essentially a **promise to pay**.
- The important thing is not merely the physical object being exchanged.
- What matters is the social recognition that:

> "You owe me, and you will pay me."

### Why does debt require society?

- If there is no mechanism for enforcing or recognizing obligations, debt becomes difficult to maintain.
- Human society therefore requires systems of:
    - trust
    - obligation
    - recognition
    - enforcement

**[Likely lecture idea:]**

> If there is no debt, there is no human society.

The point is probably that human relationships are built around **promises, obligations, and expectations**, not merely physical exchanges.

---

# 6. The Third Party / Authority

For a promise or debt to function reliably, there needs to be some **third-party authority** that can:

- witness the agreement
- recognize the obligation
- enforce the obligation
- punish violations

This moves the relationship beyond:

> Person A ↔ Person B

into something like:

> Person A ↔ **Institution/Authority** ↔ Person B

### Example: Money

Money depends on institutional trust.

- A bank or monetary institution guarantees that money has recognized value.
- The **central bank** plays an important role in maintaining the monetary system.

Your note:

> "Ang salaping ito ay bayarin ng Bangko Sentral"

This appears to be pointing toward the idea that **money is ultimately an institutional claim/obligation**, rather than simply an inherently valuable object.

# 7. State, Trust, and Guarantee

This leads to an important problem:

> **If the state guarantees the system, who guarantees the state?**

There has to be some level of **trust**.

The state itself depends on:

- institutions
- laws
- recognition
- collective trust
- enforcement mechanisms

So there is a chain:

**Individual → Society → Institution → State → Trust**

But the question remains:

> What ultimately guarantees the guarantor?

This reveals that social systems cannot be based entirely on some final physical guarantee. There is always an element of **trust and belief in the system**.

# 8. Relational Modes and the State

- Different relationships should have different **relational modes**.
- Not every kind of relationship should be mediated by the state.

### Example

You should get:

- **love** from friends and family
- **care** from personal relationships
- **legal protection/services** from the state

Rather than expecting the state to replace personal relationships.

**Key distinction:**

> The state should not become the substitute for family, friendship, or intimate relationships.

This is why different relationships need to remain **distinct and properly mediated**.

# 9. Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real (Lacanian Terms)

The **Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real (ISR)** are three different ways of understanding how something exists or operates within a relationship.

### Important idea

Each **mediating term** has an:

- Imaginary dimension
- Symbolic dimension
- Real dimension

For example:

> A friend has an Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real.

> The state also has an Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real.

These are **not three separate objects**. They are three dimensions/registers through which a relationship or social structure operates.

---

# 10. The Imaginary

### Imaginary = image, perception, identification

- Not every relationship you have with people is directly or physically "real."
- Society has an **imaginary dimension**.
- We have images and ideas of other people even without directly interacting with them.

### Examples

- You don't have to personally know everyone in society.
- You don't have to check in on everyone.
- You don't have to be close to everyone.
- You can have an idea/image of people you've never met.

A lot of life is:

> **virtual but still socially real.**

For example, you can recognize someone as:

- a professor
- a student
- a citizen
- a friend
- a stranger

without having an intimate relationship with them.

### Why does this matter?

The **lipunan remains stable partly because we don't need direct personal relationships with everyone**.

We can operate based on shared images and assumptions about other people.

---

# 11. The Symbolic

### Symbolic = rules, language, structures, meanings

The Symbolic exists through **well-defined systems and rules**.

Examples:

- mathematics
- language
- laws
- social roles
- cultural norms

A mathematical formula works because its symbols have defined relationships.

Likewise, society works because people understand shared rules.

### Relationships have rules

For a friendship to exist:

- it has to be **reciprocal**.
- You cannot continually take while giving nothing back.

If one person:

> takes → takes → takes

without reciprocity, the relationship eventually breaks down.

### Culture and Unspoken Rules

You belong to a culture not merely because you know its explicit rules.

You also need to understand its **unspoken rules**.

This is where your note about:

### Cultural Darwinism

comes in.

- People who cannot understand the **unspoken rules** of a culture may struggle to function within it.
- Social competence involves being able to recognize things that aren't explicitly stated.

---

# 12. Rules Have Leeway

Rules are important, but following rules **robotically** isn't necessarily the same as understanding them.

- There are rules.
- There are also **violations/exceptions to rules**.
- There is often **leeway** within a rule.

This is where **diskarte** comes in.

### Diskarte

- Knowing how to navigate a situation.
- Understanding when and how a rule can be interpreted or adapted.
- Knowing the appropriate thing to do beyond simply following instructions.

This connects to the French concept:

### Savoir-faire

Literally:

> **"to know how to do."**

Meaning:

- practical know-how
- knowing how to act
- knowing how to handle a situation

So:

**Knowing the rule ≠ knowing how to operate within the rule.**

---

# 13. Example: Selling

Your professor's example about selling fits here.

When selling something:

- **The buyer should be the main character.**
- The seller's job is to facilitate the buyer's needs.

If the interaction becomes primarily about:

> "Please buy this from me."

rather than:

> "How can this help you?"

then the seller can appear to be **begging** rather than selling.

The skill is understanding the **unspoken relational rules** of selling.

That's **savoir-faire/diskarte**.

---

# 14. The Real

### Real = what cannot be completely captured by the Imaginary or Symbolic

The Real is **not simply "reality."**

In Lacanian terms, it refers to something that cannot be fully represented or explained through our existing images and symbolic structures.

- It operates within relationships.
- It appears where our existing understanding reaches its limit.
- It is the aspect of an experience that **cannot be completely explained**.

Your notes capture this well:

> "Huh, shit."

The Real is the moment where you encounter something that makes you **pause because your existing framework doesn't quite work anymore**.

---

# 15. The Point of Disconnection

- The **point of disconnection** is where the Real appears.
- There is always a **limit** to our symbolic systems.
- Not everything can be perfectly explained, categorized, or represented.

So you might experience:

> "Shit, this is real, man."

Meaning you've encountered something that breaks through your normal symbolic/imaginary understanding.

# 17. Big Picture: Lacanian ISR

The three registers can be remembered like this:

|Register|Basic idea|In society|
|---|---|---|
|**Imaginary**|Images, perceptions, identification|How we imagine/recognize ourselves and others|
|**Symbolic**|Language, rules, structures|Laws, culture, language, social norms|
|**Real**|What exceeds our explanations|The limit, rupture, or thing that cannot be fully symbolized|

### Simple example: Friendship

**Imaginary**

- "This person is my friend."
- Your image/perception of the friendship.

**Symbolic**

- The expectations and rules of friendship.
- Reciprocity, trust, cultural norms.

**Real**

- Something happens that cannot easily be explained.
- The relationship reaches a limit or breaks from your expectations.
