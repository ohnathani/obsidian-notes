## Phase 1: The Mechanical Era (ca. 3000 BCE–1940)
---
Manual input, uses gears, emergence of symbolic, programmable automation.

| Year         | Milestones                            | Architectural Abstraction & Physical Implementation                                                                                                                  |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ca. 3000 BCE | **The Abacus**                        | The earliest recorded physical aid used to automate basic arithmetic and counting systems.                                                                           |
| 1614/1617    | **Napier's Bones**                    | John Napier invents mathematical logarithms and introduces rods to simplify multiplication.                                                                          |
| 1623         | **Schickard's Calculating Clock**     | Wilhelm Schickard reports a six-digit adding/multiplying gear machine with automatic bell overflow.                                                                  |
| 1642         | **The Pascaline**                     | Blaise Pascal builds the first operational gear-driven mechanical calculator featuring automated gravity-assisted carry.                                             |
| 1659         | **Burattini's 9-Wheel Machine**       | Tito Livio Burattini builds a 9-wheel calculation device paired with external manual carry rings.                                                                    |
| 1666         | **Morland's Adding Machine**          | Samuel Morland designs a mechanical cash adder and separate multiplying discs based on Napier's bones.                                                               |
| 1671         | **Leibniz's Stepped Reckoner**        | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz designs a sliding-gear stepped cylinder machine capable of all four arithmetic functions.                                                  |
| 1709         | **Poleni's Pinwheel Calculator**      | Giovanni Poleni builds the first operational calculating clock made of wood that automates multiplication.                                                           |
| 1727         | **Braun's Table Clock Calculator**    | Anton Braun presents a cylindrical, finely decorated steel, silver, and brass four-operation machine.                                                                |
| 1730         | **Hillerin de Boistissandeau**        | The French Academy of Sciences certifies three machines utilizing single-tooth and spring-loaded carry mechanisms.                                                   |
| 1770         | **Hahn's Circular Calculators**       | Philipp Matthäus Hahn builds circular calculating machines based on Leibniz's stepped cylinders.                                                                     |
| 1775         | **Stanhope's Pinwheel Machine**       | Lord Stanhope designs a pinwheel machine in a rectangular box and a Logic Demonstrator device to solve formal logic.                                                 |
| 1784         | **Müller's Circular Machine**         | Johann-Helfrich Müller builds a circular calculating machine highly similar to Hahn's design.                                                                        |
| 1804         | **The Jacquard Loom**                 | Joseph Marie Jacquard introduces textile looms controlled by punched cards, establishing stored binary instructions.                                                 |
| 1820         | **Thomas' Arithmometer**              | Charles Xavier Thomas de Colmar invents the first sturdy, commercially successful desktop mechanical calculator.                                                     |
| 1822         | **Babbage Difference Engine**         | Charles Babbage presents a small cogwheel assembly that demonstrates finite differences to compute tables.                                                           |
| 1834         | **The Analytical Engine Concept**     | Babbage begins designing a general-purpose programmable machine with separated arithmetical processing (**mill**) and storage (**store**).                           |
| 1843         | **Lovelace's Notes & First Program**  | Ada Lovelace translates and extensively annotates Luigi Menabrea's French description of the Analytical Engine, writing an algorithm to calculate Bernoulli numbers. |
| 1855         | **Scheutzian Difference Engine**      | Per Georg Scheutz completes a piano-sized working difference engine capable of calculating and printing log tables.                                                  |
| 1875         | **Wiberg's Difference Engine**        | Martin Wiberg redesigns the difference engine into a compact version the size of a sewing machine.                                                                   |
| 1887         | **Felt's Comptometer**                | Dorr E. Felt patents the first key-driven adding machine, calculating instantly upon key depression.                                                                 |
| 1890         | **Hollerith's Tabulator**             | Herman Hollerith develops an electric punched-card system for the 1890 US Census, founding the precursor of IBM.                                                     |
| 1890         | **Odhner's Arithmometer**             | W. T. Odhner manufactures a pinwheel-based redesign of Thomas' calculator with a smaller footprint and lower cost.                                                   |
| 1893         | **The 'Millionaire' Calculator**      | Introduced as the first calculator allowing direct multiplication by any digit via a mechanical product lookup table.                                                |
| 1910         | **Analytical Engine Mill Prototype**  | Henry Prevost Babbage completes a portion of the Analytical Engine's mill and printing apparatus, calculating multiples of pi.                                       |
| 1920         | **Torres' Electromechanical Machine** | Leonardo Torres Quevedo demonstrates an electromechanical calculator connected to a typewriter to print results.                                                     |

## Phase 2: The Electronic & Vacuum Tube Era (1940–1956)
---
Transitioning from mechanical friction to electronic digital speed, introducing stored programs and compilers.

| Year | Vacuum Tube Milestones & Platforms | Architectural Abstraction & Physical Implementation                                                                          |
| ---- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1941 | **Zuse Z3 (Germany)**              | Konrad Zuse builds the first functional, program-controlled, binary, Turing-complete electromechanical computer.             |
| 1942 | **Atanasoff-Berry Computer (ABC)** | Atanasoff and Clifford Berry test the first electronic digital computer using binary math and regenerative capacitor memory. |
| 1943 | **Colossus Mark 1 (UK)**           | The British military deploys Flowers' vacuum-tube logical analyzer to crack complex German Lorenz wartime ciphers.           |
| 1944 | **Harvard Mark I (IBM ASCC)**      | Howard Aiken designs the massive electromechanical calculator using decimal representation.                                  |
| 1945 | **Von Neumann's EDVAC Draft**      | John von Neumann formalizes stored-program architecture, uniting program instructions and data inside a single memory.       |
| 1945 | **Plankalkül Language**            | Konrad Zuse works on Plankalkül (Plan Calculus), the first theoretical high-level algorithmic programming language.          |
| 1946 | **ENIAC Completed**                | Mauchly and Eckert complete the 30-ton, 18,000-vacuum-tube ENIAC, the first general-purpose fully electronic computer.       |
| 1947 | **The Solid-State Transistor**     | Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley invent the point-contact transistor at Bell Labs, starting the microelectronics revolution.  |
| 1948 | **Manchester Baby Successful Run** | The first fully electronic stored-program digital computer successfully runs a factoring algorithm on June 21.               |
| 1948 | **Shannon's Communication Theory** | Claude Shannon defines the **bit** as the fundamental unit of information and a basic constraint of computation.             |
| 1949 | **EDSAC Completed (UK)**           | Maurice Wilkes builds the first practical, stored-program computer offering regular computing services to a wide community.  |
| 1949 | **Short Code Developed**           | John Mauchly designs Short Code, an early interpreted language that parses mathematical expressions.                         |
| 1951 | **A-0 Compiler Completed**         | Grace Hopper completes A-0, the first operational compiler that abstracts programming from raw machine code.                 |
| 1951 | **UNIVAC I Delivered**             | The first commercially produced general-purpose electronic digital computer is delivered to the US Census Bureau.            |
| 1953 | **Manchester Transistor Computer** | First working prototype transistorized computer, utilizing 92 point-contact transistors and 550 diodes.                      |
| 1955 | **First OS Precursors**            | General Motors and MIT develop custom Tape Director systems to automate batch processing on mainframe systems.               |

## Phase 3: Silicon & Mainframe Scaling (1956–1970s)
---
Solid-state miniaturization, integrated circuits, timesharing operating systems, and early networking.

|Year|Solid-State & Operating System Progress|Architectural Abstraction & Physical Implementation|
|---|---|---|
|1956|**GM-NAA I/O (IBM 704 OS)**|First standardized batch operating system released commercially to manage job queues automatically.|
|1957|**FORTRAN Released**|IBM's John Backus team develops high-level **FORmula TRANslation**, the standard for scientific and technical computing.|
|1958|**The Integrated Circuit (IC)**|Jack Kilby (TI) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild) independently invent the monolithic silicon microchip.|
|1958|**LISP Language**|John McCarthy develops List Processing, establishing the fundamental programming paradigm for Artificial Intelligence.|
|1959|**COBOL Conceived**|Grace Hopper and Mary K. Hawes help create Common Business Oriented Language, standardizing business databases.|
|1961|**Timesharing Prototyped (CTSS)**|MIT demonstrates the Compatible Time-Sharing System, allowing multiple concurrent users.|
|1964|**IBM System/360 Mainframes**|IBM introduces a compatible family of mainframe architectures, establishing business computing standards.|
|1964|**BASIC Language Released**|Kemeny and Kurtz design the Beginner's All-purpose Symbolic Instruction Code to democratize program writing.|
|1964|**Bell's Inequality**|John Stewart Bell mathematically proves that quantum entanglement is an experimentally falsifiable claim, not philosophy.|
|1969|**ARPANET Launch**|The US DoD deploys the first packet-switching network, establishing structural and protocol foundations of the Internet.|
|1969|**UNIX Operating System**|Thompson and Ritchie develop the UNIX operating system at Bell Labs, bringing multitasking and system portability.|
|1971|**Intel 4004 Microprocessor**|Faggin, Hoff, and Shima put an entire 4-bit CPU onto a single silicon chip containing 2,300 transistors using silicon-gate MOS technology.|
|1972|**C Programming Language**|Dennis Ritchie designs C for UNIX, creating the foundational compiled language for operating systems.|
|1973|**Xerox Alto Personal Computer**|Xerox PARC designs the first personal workstation with a GUI, mouse, bit-mapped screen, and Ethernet networking.|
|1973|**ENIAC Patent Invalidated**|U.S. District Court rules in _Honeywell v. Sperry Rand_, concluding ENIAC derived its electronic concept from Atanasoff.|
|1974|**CP/M Operating System**|Gary Kildall develops the first platform-independent, hardware-abstracted OS for microcomputers.|

## Phase 4: Personalization, Connectivity, & AI (1980s–Present)
---
From centralized mainframes to local microcomputers, global networking, elastic cloud infrastructure, and cognitive AI.

| Year  | Ubiquitous Computing, Web & AI      | Architectural Abstraction & Physical Implementation                                                                             |
| ----- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1975  | **MITS Altair 8800 Kit**            | Roberts releases the Intel 8080 kit with an open S-100 bus, launching the personal computer industry.                           |
| 1976  | **Apple I Completed**               | Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs package a single-board computer motherboard powered by the MOS 6502.                               |
| 1977  | **The Personal Computer "Trinity"** | The preassembled, consumer-ready Apple II, Commodore PET, and Tandy TRS-80 computers standardize user access.                   |
| 1979  | **VisiCalc (Apple II)**             | The first electronic spreadsheet automates recalculation, turning personal computers into vital business tools.                 |
| 1979  | **USENET Established**              | Truscott and Ellis link Duke and UNC-Chapel Hill via UUCP, creating online discussion newsgroups.                               |
| 1981  | **IBM PC Model 5150**               | IBM introduces the personal computer running Microsoft's MS-DOS, standardizing modular desktop architecture.                    |
| 1984  | **Apple Macintosh Released**        | Apple popularizes graphical user interfaces, overlapping windows, and the mouse for mass consumer audiences.                    |
| 1987  | **Perl Language Written**           | Larry Wall writes Perl to handle report extraction, serving as the "duct tape of the Internet."                                 |
| 1989  | **World Wide Web Proposed**         | Tim Berners-Lee invents HTML, HTTP, and the first browser-editor at CERN, overlaying global hypermedia onto the Internet.       |
| 1991  | **Linux Kernel Released**           | Linus Torvalds releases the open-source Linux kernel, triggering collaborative operating system scaling.                        |
| 1993  | **PDF Format Released**             | Adobe designs a universal format preserving visual document layouts across disparate systems.                                   |
| 1999  | **Salesforce SaaS Model**           | Salesforce delivers enterprise software applications over a web browser, validating Software-as-a-Service.                      |
| 2006  | **AWS S3 & EC2 Launches**           | Amazon Web Services launches Simple Storage Service and Elastic Compute Cloud, initiating elastic cloud-as-a-utility.           |
| 2007  | **The Smartphone Revolution**       | Apple releases the iPhone, transitioning general-purpose computing to pocket-sized, sensor-rich environments.                   |
| 2010s | **Hyperscale Cloud & AI**           | Centralized, massive data center networks dynamically scale computational workloads and AIOps.                                  |
| 2020s | **The Generative AI Era**           | Transformer networks and Large Language Models transition computing from strict execution to cognitive, generative interaction. |

## Parallel Trajectory: The Quantum Computing Revolution (1900–2026)
---
Exposing quantum states—qubits, superposition, and entanglement—as a computational substrate to solve intractable mathematical limits.

| Year | Quantum Milestone & Breakthrough | Architectural Abstraction & Physical Implementation                                                                                            |
| ---- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1900 | **Quantum Hypothesis**           | Max Planck introduces energy quantisation to explain black-body radiation, seeding quantum theory.                                             |
| 1964 | **Bell's Inequality Proof**      | John Bell publishes his inequality, making quantum entanglement an experimentally falsifiable claim.                                           |
| 1969 | **Conjugate Coding**             | Stephen Wiesner outlines conjugate coding, providing the conceptual foundation for quantum cryptography.                                       |
| 1973 | **Reversible Computation**       | Charles Bennett proves classical computation can be made reversible, a crucial constraint for quantum logic gates.                             |
| 1980 | **Quantum Automata Proposed**    | Yuri Manin outlines using quantum states to represent information in his monograph _Computable and Noncomputable_.                             |
| 1980 | **First Quantum Turing Model**   | Paul Benioff publishes the first formal quantum-mechanical model of a Turing machine.                                                          |
| 1981 | **Feynman's Keynote (MIT)**      | Richard Feynman declares nature isn't classical, demanding quantum hardware to simulate natural quantum physical systems.                      |
| 1984 | **BB84 Key Distribution**        | Bennett and Brassard establish quantum cryptography by designing the BB84 protocol.                                                            |
| 1985 | **Universal Quantum Computer**   | David Deutsch mathematically formalizes the universal quantum Turing machine and defines quantum parallel logic gates.                         |
| 1992 | **Deutsch-Jozsa Algorithm**      | The first quantum algorithm demonstrating a provable exponential speedup over classical algorithms.                                            |
| 1994 | **Shor's Factoring Algorithm**   | Peter Shor publishes a polynomial-time integer factoring algorithm, proving quantum hardware can break RSA encryption.                         |
| 1995 | **Trapped-Ion CNOT Gate**        | Cirac & Zoller propose and Monroe & Wineland demonstrate the first physical 2-qubit CNOT gate on a beryllium ion.                              |
| 1995 | **Quantum Error Correction**     | Shor and Steane independently design QEC codes, proving fragile qubits can be protected from decoherence.                                      |
| 1996 | **Grover's Search Algorithm**    | Lov Grover develops a search algorithm providing a quadratic speedup for unstructured databases.                                               |
| 1997 | **Toric Code Proposal**          | Alexei Kitaev proposes fault-tolerant topological quantum computation, the ancestor of modern surface codes.                                   |
| 1999 | **Superconducting Qubit**        | Yasunobu Nakamura and team demonstrate the first solid-state superconducting charge qubit at NEC.                                              |
| 2001 | **Shor Factored 15**             | Vandersypen and Chuang use Shor's algorithm on a 7-qubit NMR system to factor 15, verifying algorithm execution.                               |
| 2011 | **First Commercial Annealer**    | D-Wave Systems sells the first commercial 128-qubit quantum annealing computer to Lockheed Martin.                                             |
| 2016 | **Cloud Quantum Experience**     | IBM launches public cloud-based access to a 5-qubit superconducting quantum computer, democratizing research.                                  |
| 2018 | **NISQ Era Defined**             | John Preskill coins the term **Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum** to define uncorrected 50-to-1,000-qubit processors.                          |
| 2019 | **Quantum Supremacy Claim**      | Google's 53-qubit Sycamore chip executes random-circuit sampling in 200 seconds, outperforming supercomputers; the claim was contested by IBM. |
| 2024 | **PQC Standards Finalized**      | NIST publishes the first three post-quantum cryptography standards: FIPS 203, FIPS 204, and FIPS 205.                                          |
| 2024 | **Google Willow QEC**            | Google's 105-qubit Willow chip demonstrates below-threshold surface-code quantum error correction.                                             |
| 2025 | **Majorana 1 & 6K-Atom**         | Microsoft announces the Majorana 1 topological chip; Caltech (Endres) assembles a 6,100-atom neutral-atom array.                               |
| 2026 | **96 Logical Qubits**            | QuEra demonstrates 96 logical qubits operating on neutral-atom arrays using a [[16,6,4]] code.                                                 |

## Insights
---
The history of computers has come a long way, thanks to the computer scientists of the past who contributed to and innovated computing devices. These devices have helped the world grow, become more modernized, and make everyday tasks easier through the use of technology. Past computer scientists invented computational devices that could perform calculations quickly, solve complex mathematical problems, and automate tedious data processing. These technologies were also used for military and scientific purposes.

Those who may not know, computing is finite; it is limited by how much your computational device can handle. Computer scientists have continuously pushed that ceiling higher, bringing us to where we are today. It all started with the invention of the abacus, and fast-forward to the present, we are now living in the modern world.

Computing has come a long, long way. For example, the brand-new iPhone 17 Pro Max is roughly 2.3 million times faster than the Apollo Guidance Computer, the computer used during the 1969 Moon landing. Another notable innovation, and my personal favorite, is IBM's Deep Blue, a famous chess computer that sparked global awareness of AI and defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a historic six-game match in 1997. Although Deep Blue is completely different from the AI we know today, such as ChatGPT, it helped pave the way for the development of modern AI, particularly predictive and machine-learning systems.

Overall, the invention and development of computers have played a pivotal role in shaping how our society has grown and will continue to grow in the future.
