## Early Computing Devices
---
- circa 3000 BCE - Abacus
	- earliest tools for math calculation
-  1642 - Pascaline
	- **Blaise Pascal** developed mechanical calculator for arithmetic
- 1820-1830s - Difference Engine
	- **Charles Babbage** designed mech machine to auto calculate math tables
- 1830s - Analytical Engine
	- Babbage designed a programmable mechanical computer, applied concepts and ideas of memory, processing, and input/output
- 1840s - Ada Lovelace
	- wrote algorithm for the Analytical Engine
	- recognized as **first computer programmer**

## Electromechanical Computers
---
- 1890 - Hollerith Tabulating Machine
	- **Herman Hollerith** used punched cards to process U.S. Census data
- 1939 -1941 - Zuse Computers
	- **Konrad Zuse** developed early programmable electromagnetic computers, including the Z3
- 1944 - Harvard Mark I
	- Large electromechanical computer used for auto calculations. (Computer History Museum, n.d.)

## First Generation: Vacuum Tubes
---
**1940s-1950s**
	- Main technology: vacuum tubes
- Characteristics
	- Very large
	- Expensive
	- Consumed enormous amounts of electricity
	- Produced significant heat
	- Required frequent maintenance
- 1946 - ENIAC
	- one of the first general-purpose electronic digital computer
	- used approx, 18,000 vacuum tubes
	- Occupied more than 1,000 sq.ft and weighed about 30 tons
	- More than 1000 times faster than previous electromechanical computers (Computer History Museum, n.d.)

## Stored-Program Computers
---
- 1948 - Manchester "Baby"
	- First program sucessfully ran on the machine on **June 21, 1948**
	- Demonstrated the use of electronic memory for storing programs
	- Important step toward modern stored-program computers. (Computer History Museum, n.d.)

## Second Generation: Transistors
---
**1950s - 1960s**
- Transistors began replacing vacuum tubes
- Advantages:
	- Smaller 
	- Faster
	- More reliable
	- Lower power consumption
	- Produced less heat
- 1953 - Manchester Transistor Computer
	- Early working transistorized computer.
	- Used 92 transistors and 550 diodes (Computer History Museum, n.d.)

## Third Generation: Integrated Circuits (ICs)
---
**1960s-1970s**
- Technology: Integrated circuits (ICs)
- Multiple electronic components could be placed onto a single chip
- Benefits:
	- Smaller computers
	- Greater processing power
	- Lower cost
	- Improved reliability
- 1960 - First planar integrated cicuit
	- Major milestone in semiconductor development
- 1962 - ICs used in aerospace computer systems
	- Smaller size and lower power consumption made ICs especially valuable (Computer History Museum, n.d.)
- 1964 - IBM System/360
	- Major computer family introduced by IBM.
	- Helped establish compatible computer architectures (Computer History Museum, n.d.)


## Fourth Generation: Microprocessors
---
1970s-present
- 1971 - Intel 4004
	- Intel introduced the 4004, the general-purpose programmable microprocessor
	- Contained approx. 2300 transistors
	- Made it possible to put processing functions onto a single programmable chip
	- Helped make computers smaller and cheaper (Intel, n.d.)

## Personal Computer Revolution
---
- 1975 - Altair 8800
	- Popularized computer kits for hobbyists
	- Helped launch the personal computer era
- 1976 - Apple I
	- Developed by **Steve Wozniak** and marketed with **Steve Jobs**
- 1977 - Apple II
	- One of the first highly successful consumer personal computers
- 1981 - IBM PC
	- IBM introduced its Personal Computer
	- Became highly influential in business and personal computing. (Computer History Museum, n.d.)

## Modern Computing
---
**1980s-present**
- 1984 - Macintosh
	- Helped popularize graphical user interfaces
- 1990s - Internet and World Wide Web
	- Computers became connected globally
- 2000s - Mobile and cloud computing
	- Laptops, smartphones, and cloud services became widespread
- 2010s-present
	- Rapid development of
		- AI
		- ML
		- GPUs
		- Smartphones
		- Cloud Compute
		- Supecomputing
		- Internet of Things
- Modern chips can contain billions of transistors compared with the small number used in early transistor computers (Computer History Museum, n.d.)
